## Summary

Evidence-based wayfinding design patterns drawn from environmental psychology research on parks, hospitals, and public buildings. Organizes findings into design patterns (WF:D1–D3) and map patterns (WF:M1–M5). Central argument: wayfinding is a cognitive problem-solving task — spatial layout must reduce cognitive load at every decision point. Signage alone cannot rescue a poorly organized plan. Maps must be user-centered, not data-complete.

## Principles

- Wayfinding is a four-phase cognitive loop: form plan → execute → monitor → re-plan. Architecture must support all four, not just provide signage at the end.
- Distinct zones (WF:D1 "Regions"): 5 coherent regions is the sweet spot; fewer than 3 reduces spatial structure; more than 7 taxes memory (p. 4, WF:D1).
- Landmarks must be both visually distinctive AND in harmony with surroundings — jarring landmarks help navigation but degrade experience (WF:D2, p. 5–6).
- Too many landmarks are as bad as none — each must be reliably identifiable from a distance (WF:D2).
- Path differentiation: primary and secondary paths distinguished by surface texture, width, and edge treatment — not by signage alone (WF:D3, p. 7).
- Posted "You-Are-Here" maps must be oriented to viewer's forward direction, not north-up; north-up posted maps consistently confuse users (WF:M4, p. 17).
- New-visitor maps should emphasize three things only: landmarks, choice points, and major regions. Omitting everything else is a feature, not a flaw (WF:M1, p. 9–11).
- Accuracy hang-up: slightly inaccurate maps that straighten paths and exaggerate topography are more usable than literal ones (WF:M2, p. 12–13).
- Labels directly on map features outperform legend-and-symbol systems; every legend lookup is a working memory interruption (WF:M3, p. 14–15).
- User testing of maps is mandatory; map designer's perspective systematically differs from first-time-user's perspective (WF:M5, p. 18).
- Color-coding and floor-specific cues must be applied consistently at every scale (entry, corridor, elevator, room) for them to function.
- Stress amplifies wayfinding failure — emergency and healthcare settings demand simpler spatial organization than retail or office.
