## Summary

Chapter 7 addresses the built environment's role in combating the global obesity epidemic and sedentary behavior. It defines active living across four behavioral domains: active transportation (walking/biking), occupational/school physical activity, recreational physical activity, and household physical activity. The chapter reviews evidence on mixed land use, street connectivity, parks, schools, workplaces, and housing, then presents the Mueller Community in Austin as a case study demonstrating measurable improvements in physical activity (+76 min MVPA/week), social health, and economic value after moving to a walkable community. Design concepts are organized as a table by domain, setting, and strategy. (p. 111–129)

## Operationalizable Principles with Page Citations

1. **Ecological model of active living.** Individual-focused obesity interventions have failed. The ecological model — which considers behavior as influenced by the physical and social environment — is the evidence-supported framework for design. Buildings and communities shape activity choices. (p. 3 / book p. 112)

2. **Mixed land use for active transportation.** Communities that bring everyday destinations (homes, workplaces, schools, shops, parks) closer together enable active transportation by walking and biking. Each additional type of destination within 400 m adds 5–6 min/week of walking. (p. 5–6 / book p. 114–115)

3. **Transit access promotes physical activity.** 29% of US public transit users achieve the 30+ min/day physical activity recommendation simply by walking to and from transit. Transit proximity is a design strategy for activity. (p. 6 / book p. 115)

4. **Sidewalk completeness and connectivity.** Sidewalks are the most important environmental correlate of adult physical activity internationally. Streets should have complete, connected sidewalks with smooth surfaces and amenities. (p. 7 / book p. 116)

5. **Park proximity standard: ≤1 mile / 1 km.** Proximity to parks is the most studied and consistently positive predictor of physical activity across adults, adolescents, and children. One mile or one kilometer is a reasonable maximum park-service distance. (p. 8 / book p. 117)

6. **Park quality attributes matter.** Trails, sports facilities, park size, aesthetics, maintenance, drinking fountains, bathrooms, and trash cans all affect park use for physical activity. Park design must address quality, not just presence. (p. 8 / book p. 117)

7. **Workplace active design features.** Prominent, well-lit stairwells with locally produced artwork, point-of-decision elevator prompts, community gyms, outdoor gardens, and fitness areas in multifamily housing all increase daily steps. (p. 10 / book p. 119)

8. **School environments: outdoor is most impactful scale.** Across desk, classroom/building, and outdoors scales, the outdoor environment has the strongest and most studied relationship with students' physical activity. Prioritize outdoor design in school projects. (p. 9 / book p. 118)

9. **Standing desks for light occupational activity.** Standing desks show positive impacts for light physical activity in occupational/school settings, though they are less effective than spatial/outdoor strategies for moderate or vigorous activity. (p. 9 / book p. 118)

10. **Activity-friendly housing design.** Active design features in affordable housing — onsite gym, visible stairs, point-of-decision prompts, outdoor gardens, playgrounds — increase residents' daily steps. These features are especially important for lower-income populations with higher health risk. (p. 10 / book p. 119)

11. **Equity in physical activity environments.** Access to parks, trails, and open spaces is often reduced in low-income and minority communities. Safe streets for pedestrians and cyclists are also reduced. Design and policy must address these disparities explicitly. (p. 11 / book p. 120)

12. **Co-benefits argument for activity-friendly design.** Activity-friendly environments generate compounding benefits across physical health, social health, safety, environmental quality, and economic value — documented at Mueller: +76 min MVPA/week, improved social cohesion, higher property values. Use co-benefits framing to build stakeholder support. (p. 11, 14–15 / book p. 120, 123–124)

13. **LEED-ND and New Urbanism as policy levers.** LEED Neighborhood Development and New Urbanism promote walkability, mixed use, and complete streets — existing certification pathways align with active living design goals. (p. 13 / book p. 122)

14. **Health impact assessment (HIA) for major projects.** HIA uses quantitative, qualitative, and participatory methods to estimate health impacts of proposed projects. Should be used for neighborhood developments and major urban interventions. (p. 13 / book p. 122)

15. **Compact community pattern is the foundational active living intervention.** Bringing everyday destinations within walkable distance is more effective than any individual building-scale feature. Community master planning decisions set the ceiling for what individual buildings can achieve. (p. 5 / book p. 114)
