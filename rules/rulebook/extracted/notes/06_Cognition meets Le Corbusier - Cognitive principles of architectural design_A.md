## Summary

Cognitive science paper applying spatial memory research to architectural design, with a focus on wayfinding. Central thesis: navigational difficulty is not caused by "geometric complexity" of a floor plan as seen from above, but by misalignment between the local reference systems occupants encounter room-by-room and the global structure of the building. Buildings with orthogonal layouts and consistent reference frames are cognitively navigable; buildings with rotated sub-sections, radial geometries, or inconsistent axis grids are not — even when their floor plans "look simple." Recommends orthogonality, salient anchor axes, and ICD index benchmarking as design tools.

## Principles

- Reference-frame alignment is the core wayfinding variable: occupants build spatial maps from individual room-scale reference frames, then integrate them. Misalignment between adjacent frames dramatically increases error and disorientation (p. 2–5).
- "You-Are-Here" maps work only when the map's up direction matches the observer's forward direction. North-up fixed maps consistently produce errors (p. 4 — alignment effect, Levine et al. 1982).
- Floor plan geometric complexity (figural complexity from above) does not predict navigational difficulty — experienced complexity (sequence of partial views from within) is the real variable (p. 8–9, O'Neill 1991).
- ICD index (Interconnection Density, O'Neill 1991): average number of paths from any decision node. Higher ICD = harder navigation. Benchmark: ICD values shown in paper range from 1.00 (simple corridor) to 1.80 (complex branching); keep ICD ≤ 1.50 for non-expert occupants (p. 7–8, Fig. 2).
- Orthogonal room layouts aligned with the building's main axes dramatically reduce the cognitive cost of integrating spatial knowledge across rooms (p. 5–6, Werner et al. 1998).
- Buildings with a section rotated 45° relative to the rest (e.g., Göttingen Rathaus elevator core) appear as a "diamond" on the plan but are perceived as a square by occupants — creating a systematic reference-frame conflict that makes the building notoriously hard to navigate (p. 11, Fig. 6–7).
- Salient environmental structure (major axes, landmarks, orthogonal grids) functions as an extrinsic reference system that all rooms can share — providing a common cognitive frame for the whole building (p. 5–6, 9–10).
- First view primacy: the first clear view of a spatial layout anchors the reference system used for all subsequent navigation (Shelton & McNamara 1997). Design the entry sequence to communicate the building's main axis and overall structure immediately (p. 4–5).
- Radial/star layouts appear legible on plans but are cognitively opaque from within — each street/corridor offers only a local reference and the global structure is invisible (p. 8–9, Minneapolis example).
- Gestalt grouping (proximity, similarity, continuity) is involuntary — zone identity perceived before occupants consciously choose to read it. Use this: consistent material + lighting + width signals zone membership automatically.
