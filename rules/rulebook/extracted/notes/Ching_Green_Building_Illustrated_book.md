## Summary

Comprehensive illustrated guide to green building design, organized around the "outside-in" methodology: resolve community and site first, then building shape, then envelope layers, then systems. Key argument is that envelope integrity — multiple continuous, robust, and maintained layers of shelter — does more for building performance than any single high-tech green feature. Defines green building as substantially reduced environmental impact AND improved indoor health conditions. Emphasizes integrated design from project inception.

## Principles

- Green building = reduced environmental impact AND indoor conditions conducive to human health — both goals are required, neither is optional (p. 14, §2).
- Outside-in methodology: design decisions should proceed from community → site → building shape → near-building features → outer envelope → unconditioned spaces → inner envelope → thermal zoning → systems. Each outer layer reduces the load the inner layer must handle (p. 18, §2.06).
- "Layers of shelter" principle: every building component is evaluated as a shelter layer against a specific load (temperature, sun, rain, wind, humidity, pests, noise). Multiple overlapping layers are more effective than one perfect layer (p. 16–17, §2.04–2.05).
- Continuity of each layer is as important as its insulating value: gaps, thermal bridges, and poorly sealed penetrations can negate a well-specified wall (p. 19–20, §2.07–2.08).
- Nonrobust layers (doors, weatherstripping, caulk) degrade over time; minimize the count of exterior doors and operable penetrations to maintain envelope performance long-term (p. 20–21, §2.09–2.10).
- Holistic design: one high-performance component (e.g., 12-in. super-insulated wall) cannot rescue a building with poor windows, leaky attic, and inefficient distribution. All losses must be minimized together (p. 21, §2.11).
- Integrated design: owner, architect, engineers, and contractors work together from project inception — sustainability decisions made early (SD phase) cost nothing; the same decisions at DD cost 10× more (p. 22, §2.12).
- Site selection: brownfield/infill reduces embodied site infrastructure impact and enables transit access vs. greenfield.
- Building orientation: long axis east-west maximizes south glazing for passive solar and minimizes east/west exposure, which is hardest to shade.
- Window-to-wall ratio (WWR): 30–40% WWR is the typical optimum for daylighting without excessive heat gain or loss; east and west facades warrant lower WWR or significant external shading.
- Toplighting (skylights, clerestories) delivers 4–8× more uniform illumination than equivalent area of sidelighting.
- EPDs (Environmental Product Declarations) must be required for all major structural and envelope materials to quantify embodied carbon.
