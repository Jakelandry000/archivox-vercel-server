## Summary

Explores evolutionary and psychological roots of why humans find certain spaces pleasurable. Draws on environmental psychology and biophilia to explain preference for spaces with prospect, refuge, mystery, and complexity. Human spatial preferences evolved for survival optimization.

## Principles

- Prospect-refuge theory: spaces offering long views with protected backs feel inherently safe and pleasant (Appleton)
- Mystery — partial concealment inviting exploration — increases aesthetic preference across most cultures
- Complexity without chaos: optimal stimulation level (Berlyne) — too simple is boring, too complex is stressful
- Coherence and legibility are prerequisites for spatial comfort in novel environments
- Biophilic elements (water, greenery, natural light) consistently raise subjective wellbeing scores
- Social density matters: overcrowding triggers stress even in otherwise well-designed spaces
- Thermal comfort overlaps with psychological safety — warmth signals belonging and shelter
- Cultural overlays modify but do not override evolved environmental preferences
- Affordance theory (Gibson): spaces 'invite' certain behaviors; good design makes affordances legible
- Enclosure + openness balance: a mix of defined and open space outperforms either extreme
