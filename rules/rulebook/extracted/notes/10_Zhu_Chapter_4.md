## Summary

Chapter 4 reviews the state of knowledge in environmental design for mental and behavioral health (MBH). One in eight people globally live with a mental illness; half will develop an MBH condition in their lifetime. The chapter surveys inpatient, outpatient, community, and non-clinical MBH environments; reviews literature on aggression, restraint, and safety outcomes; introduces trauma-informed design (TID); presents the EmPATH emergency psychiatric care model; and applies these principles to educational settings (Sandy Hook redesign). The CHIME framework (Connectedness, Hope, Identity, Meaning, Empowerment) provides a structured set of design expressions for MBH environments. (p. 56–73)

## Operationalizable Principles with Page Citations

1. **MBH principles apply beyond psychiatric facilities.** Generic healthcare, schools, housing, workplaces, and retail all benefit from MBH design principles. The charge to support mental health is not limited to psychiatric settings. (p. 4 / book p. 58)

2. **MBH facilities: small units and mixed room types.** Research supports community-located, small-unit MBH facilities with a mix of single- and double-bed rooms, communal areas, open nurse stations, plants, furniture clusters, and a public-private balance. (p. 8 / book p. 62 — Jovanovic et al., 2019)

3. **Trauma-informed design (TID) principles:** TID includes reducing environmental stressors, supporting self-reliance and dignity, promoting connection to nature, separating people from those in distress, and providing opportunities for choice. (p. 6 / book p. 60)

4. **EmPATH: open floor plan and staff integration.** The EmPATH emergency psychiatric care model uses an open floor plan allowing patient movement, food and clothing access, recliner seating (not beds), and integrated staff-patient milieu rather than isolated nurses' stations. (p. 11 / book p. 65)

5. **EmPATH: outdoor views for high-acuity patients.** The EmPATH unit provides chairs with outdoor views (Figure 4.5). Views of nature are a design goal even in emergency psychiatric settings. (p. 11 / book p. 65)

6. **Eliminate nurses' station "fishbowl" design.** The physical and symbolic separation created by enclosed nurses' stations is identified as a negative design element by both staff and patients. Open, integrated station designs are preferred in MBH environments. (p. 14 / book p. 68)

7. **CHIME framework for MBH environment programming.** Jenkin et al.'s interviews of 85 residents and staff yielded design expressions for Connectedness, Hope/Optimism, Identity, Meaning/Purpose, and Empowerment. Each concept maps to specific spatial provisions — community rooms, personal spaces, skill areas, gardens. (p. 14–15 / book p. 68–69)

8. **MBH empowerment: user environmental control.** Patients' empowerment is supported by increased environmental control over temperature, sound, and light — design should provide local adjustment capability in occupied zones. (p. 15 / book p. 69)

9. **Trauma-informed school design: natural materials, outdoor space, daylight.** The Sandy Hook redesign incorporated natural materials, ample outdoor space, and enhanced daylight access through interior and exterior windows to support trauma recovery and community healing. (p. 12–13 / book p. 66–67)

10. **Student/community ownership through art integration.** Children at Sandy Hook created artwork displayed in the new school. Involving occupants in creating art reinforces ownership and psychological investment. (p. 13 / book p. 67)

11. **MBH negative design factors to avoid:** Small spaces, blind spots, limited exits, poor adjacencies, inadequate visibility, restricted alarm access, and poor indoor climate are identified by staff as negative. Confined spaces and poor nurse stations are flagged by patients. (p. 9 / book p. 63 — Jenkin et al., 2022)

12. **Alternative to seclusion and restraint:** Rather than seclusion rooms, provide gardens, recreation facilities, sensory rooms, and private, low-density, peaceful spaces as de-escalation environments. (p. 8 / book p. 62 — Oostermeijer et al., 2021)

13. **Housing instability worsens MBH outcomes.** Houseless youth are 11× more likely than housed peers to be admitted to inpatient MBH facilities. Permanent supportive housing is a design health intervention. (p. 5 / book p. 59)

14. **Design for all MBH facility types consistently.** MBH care is provided in hospitals, freestanding facilities, military/VA systems, and community organizations. Design principles should be applied consistently across all settings, not only flagship psychiatric hospitals. (p. 5 / book p. 59)

15. **Assess MBH design rigorously despite variable control challenges.** Validity of single-site findings is strengthened when consistent with prior studies. Rely on converging evidence; don't dismiss EBD because of methodological limits inherent to occupied environments. (p. 7 / book p. 61)
