## Summary

Hall's proxemics theory establishes four culturally-modulated distance zones (intimate, personal, social, public) that architecture must respect. Violations of proxemic norms create discomfort and avoidance behaviors.

## Principles

- Intimate distance (0-18in / 0-45cm): reserved for close relationships; do not force strangers into this zone
- Personal distance (1.5-4ft / 45-120cm): casual interaction; appropriate for small offices and residential dining
- Social distance (4-12ft / 1.2-3.6m): impersonal business; typical office desk-to-visitor spacing
- Public distance (12ft+ / 3.6m+): formal settings; lecture halls, stages, ceremonial spaces
- Fixed-feature space (walls, structure) sets hard limits; semi-fixed (furniture) can be tuned by occupants
- Crowding stress is not just density but inability to control interpersonal distance
- Cultural variation: design for the most space-sensitive user in multi-cultural contexts
- Acoustic privacy often matters as much as visual: sound masking addresses proxemic needs
- Furniture arrangement is architecture: row seating inhibits conversation; angled clusters promote it
- Informal space (unconscious personal bubbles) must be legible to designers to avoid friction
