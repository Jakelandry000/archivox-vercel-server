## Summary

Chapter 3 maps nine pathways through which the built environment affects physical health: ergonomics, air quality, thermal comfort, water quality, sound environment, light, views of and access to nature, safety and security, and opportunities for physical activity. For each domain, the authors review evidence from architecture/interior design and landscape architecture/urban planning, then present integrated design strategies. The chapter ends with a table of health-focused rating systems (WELL v2, Fitwel, LEED-ND) and calls for integrated approaches that blur the indoor/outdoor boundary. (p. 31–55)

## Operationalizable Principles with Page Citations

1. **Ergonomic kitchen work triangle:** Minimize distance between fridge, oven, and sink to reduce movement, injury risk, and workflow inefficiency. This "golden triangle" principle applies to any repetitive-task workstation. (p. 6 / book p. 34)

2. **User-engaged ergonomics:** Because activity types vary significantly across workplaces, designers must involve end users to ensure ergonomic design that maximizes safety, efficiency, and comfort. (p. 7 / book p. 35)

3. **Low-VOC and formaldehyde-free material specification:** Buildings should specify no-VOC paints, formaldehyde-free products, and avoid scented cleaning agents. This applies to all occupied spaces, not just healthcare. (p. 8 / book p. 36)

4. **Vegetative air quality buffers near traffic and industrial zones:** Green walls and vegetative buffers near high-traffic areas intercept airborne particulates and mitigate respiratory health risk. Trees absorb CO2 and release O2. (p. 9 / book p. 37)

5. **Passive thermal comfort design priority:** Environmental factors — air temperature, radiant temperature, humidity, air velocity — all affect thermal comfort. Passive strategies (orientation, shading, ventilation, green roofs) should be tried before mechanical solutions. (p. 10 / book p. 38)

6. **Cool pavement in hot urban zones:** Cool pavements in pedestrian areas and parks can reduce neighborhood surface temperatures by up to 10°F (5.5°C), improving thermal comfort during heat waves. (p. 12 / book p. 40)

7. **Street wind management:** Prevailing wind directions should govern building location and street alignment to maximize summer ventilation and reduce cold-season wind hazards. (p. 12 / book p. 40)

8. **Acoustic noise control hierarchy: source → absorption → blocking → masking.** Design teams should address noise in this sequence: avoid noisy locations, select quiet equipment, use absorptive furnishings, install solid walls, then use masking as a last resort. (p. 15 / book p. 43)

9. **WHO hospital noise limits as general standard:** WHO guidelines recommend ≤30 dBA background and ≤40 dBA peak at night in patient wards. These thresholds are applicable as targets for any sensitive occupied space (residential, offices, classrooms). (p. 15 / book p. 43)

10. **Daylight as a physical health resource:** Interaction with daylight is linked to improved vision, reduced eye strain, reduced headaches, stimulated circadian physiology, improved sleep, reduced ADHD symptoms, and reduced depression. Maximize daylight access in design. (p. 16 / book p. 44)

11. **Night lighting for activity without pollution:** Nighttime lighting enables physical and social activity after dark, but requires downlighting, motion sensors, and warm-colored lamps to mitigate light pollution and protect circadian rhythms. (p. 16 / book p. 44)

12. **Diversify nature contact types:** Design should provide a range — from passive viewing (window views, pictures) to physical presence in nature (courtyard, rooftop garden) to active interaction (gardening). Multiple modes compound benefit. (p. 18 / book p. 46)

13. **Equitable greenspace distribution:** Urban planning policies should prioritize sufficient and equitable access to green space. Proximity to parks or recreation settings is the most consistently and positively studied attribute for physical activity across age groups. (p. 19 / book p. 47)

14. **Safety by design beyond physical security:** Safety encompasses cybersecurity, fire, active-shooter events, and business continuity. Perceived threat triggers stress responses with direct health consequences. (p. 19 / book p. 47–48)

15. **Integrated health rating system compliance:** WELL v2, Fitwel, and LEED-ND provide validated health-focused performance frameworks. Projects should check compliance across all ten WELL concepts: air, water, nourishment, light, movement, thermal comfort, sound, materials, mind, and community. (p. 21 / book p. 49)
